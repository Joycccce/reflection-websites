The question followed me into my master’s degree.

Since I started my master’s degree, I have often asked myself one question: what is special about a designer? Why does the world need designers? Why do companies need them?

This question is not easy to answer. Many people solve problems in their jobs. Engineers, businesspeople, scientists, and managers all solve problems. They talk to people, make decisions, and create new things. If I only say that designers are creative problem-solvers, it does not explain what makes design different.

A conversation

An engineer once asked me, “What is an integrated designer?” He wanted a clear answer, not a broad promise.

I used my current green-building project to explain what I meant. I said, “You design a better energy system. I design a better way of living around it.”

Then he asked how I could prove it.

He replied, “So is design just general problem-solving? It sounds very abstract. How can you measure the relationship between people and a building?”

I was stuck. I believed that the relationship was real and important. But I could not explain how we could see it, test it, or improve it. My answer was too simple. I had said that engineers focus on energy while designers focus on people.

After thinking about it again, I saw that this was not fair. Engineers also care about people. Designers also need to understand technology, money, materials, and systems.

The difference may be how we connect the whole situation.

A green building is not only an energy machine. It is also a place where people work, study, rest, and meet. At the same time, engineers do not only care about machines. They also think about safety, comfort, cost, and the people who use the building.

Design class helped me find a more exact answer. We learned about designerly ways of knowing. Design does not have only one subject. Designers may work with buildings, services, products, software, organisations, or public systems.

What makes design different is also how designers work. They make models of possible situations. They bring different kinds of knowledge together. They turn ideas into something people can see, use, discuss, and change.

A building starts another life when people enter it.

When engineers finish installing an energy system, real people begin to use the building every day. They may not use it in the way the technical team expected. They may misunderstand the system, ignore it, resist it, or find their own way to use it.

The building may save energy according to the technical data, but people may still feel hot, confused, or unable to control their environment. The system may work well on paper but not work well in daily life.

This is where a designer can help. Experience does not need to be only a soft or unclear feeling. We can collect evidence about it and connect that evidence with technical information.

A good designer does not pretend to know.

A good designer should not speak for people without evidence. Designers can make wrong guesses. They need to listen, observe, test, and change their ideas.

This may also explain why companies need designers. A product or system may work well from a technical view but still fail in the real world. People may not understand it, trust it, or know how to use it. It may not fit into daily life.

Still, not every problem needs a designer. A designer creates value when they help a team find the right problem before building the wrong answer. They connect information that was separated before and make hidden problems easier to see.

What I would tell the engineer now.

“An integrated designer does not only solve more kinds of problems. The designer studies the relationship between people, technology, space, and time. We make this relationship visible through research and prototypes. Then we study how it changes both people’s experience and the performance of the system.”

This answer is still not perfect. I am still learning what it means to be a designer. But it is clearer than saying that engineers focus on energy while designers focus on people.

How do I make sense of the data gathered?

In our gym-equipment project, we used four methods to move from raw data to insights. First, journey mapping helped us organise each participant’s movements, waiting time, comments and decisions into a sequence. Second, behavioural coding allowed us to classify responses such as waiting, asking, sharing, switching equipment, reordering the workout or leaving. Third, through cross-case comparison, we compared different participants and situations, including peak and off-peak periods and actual versus perceived waiting time. Finally, theme development helped us group repeated patterns into three early themes: uncertainty, plan rigidity and social cues.

I cannot say that design is always important. But when a system works and people still cannot live well with it, a good designer is needed.
