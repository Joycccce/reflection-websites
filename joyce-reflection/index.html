Design × AI
as a collaborator

Human judgement
still matters

I use AI most often when my thoughts are messy. I may have many notes, interview quotes, observations, and different ideas at the same time. AI helps me put them together, see possible links, and explain what I am thinking more clearly. That is why I see AI as a collaborator. It works with me, but it should not make the final decision for me.

A good example is our Yusof Ishak House project at NUS. At the beginning, we found something strange: many students said YIH was too hot, but there were also air-conditioned rooms in the building, and some of them were empty. If I ask AI why students do not use these spaces, it can quickly give many reasonable answers. Maybe students do not know the rooms exist. Maybe the rules are unclear. Maybe the spaces do not feel welcoming. Maybe students simply prefer somewhere else.

These answers are useful because they give us ideas to check. But they are still only guesses. They can sound smart without being true.

“I didn’t know I was allowed to go in.”

This sentence came from a real student interview, and it changed our whole way of looking at the problem. Before that, we were asking, “Why don’t students like YIH?” After the interview, the question became, “Do students even know that they are allowed to use these spaces?”

Later, we found more differences between different groups. Some student clubs had their own air-conditioned rooms, while ordinary students were often not sure which rooms they could enter or book. That kind of detail came from real people and real situations. AI could help us organise it, but it could not discover it for us.

This made me realise that AI is very good at creating a possible explanation based on patterns it already knows. It can help me compare ideas, find patterns, or question my framing. But real people give something different: experience, emotion, context, and surprises.

So I would never let AI handle user insight, final judgement, ethical decisions, or emotional understanding by itself. The same is true for desk research. If AI gives me a market number, research result, or case study, I still need the original source. I only use the information after I check the evidence myself.

For me, the rule is simple: AI can help me understand evidence, but it should not decide what counts as evidence. It is useful because it helps me think faster and more clearly. But understanding the real world still needs observation, interviews, direct evidence, and human judgement.

AI helps me make sense of things.
It can organise, compare, question and expand ideas — but it should not replace direct evidence.

What AI cannot own

User insight, final judgement, ethical decisions, emotional understanding, and the final check on whether information is trustworthy.

One rule

Reasonable is not the same as real.
AI can give a plausible answer. Research still needs evidence.

AI gives me

possible explanations, quick structure, patterns, comparisons, clearer wording, and questions I can test.

Real people give me

lived experience, emotion, context, contradictions, and unexpected insights that can change the direction of the project.
